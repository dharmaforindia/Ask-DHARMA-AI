# Oral advocacy and trial craft

## Preparing for a hearing

The night before, you should be able to answer these questions without notes:

1. What exactly do I want the court to order today? (The operative words.)
2. What is the one point that gets me there?
3. What are the two facts the bench must know, and where are they in the record?
4. Which authority decides the point, and which paragraph?
5. What is the other side's best point, and what is my answer in two sentences?
6. What is the bench likely to ask? (Write down ten questions and your answers.)
7. What will I concede?
8. If the bench is against me, what is my fall-back prayer?

Build a **Hearing Kit**:

- A one-page argument note (short point, propositions, authorities with paragraphs).
- A compilation of authorities with the relevant paragraphs flagged or highlighted, plus an index.
  Give copies to the bench and the other side.
- A convenience compilation of the key record pages, if the paper book is large.
- The draft operative order you want.

## Structure of an oral argument

1. **Opening (30 to 60 seconds).** "My Lords, I appear for the petitioner. This petition challenges
   ___. The short point is ___. I seek ___."
2. **Facts (1 to 3 minutes).** Only the facts the decision turns on, with page numbers. Do not read
   the whole petition.
3. **Propositions.** Give each one as a heading, then the provision, then the authority with its
   paragraph, then how it applies here. Start with the strongest.
4. **The other side's answer.** Deal with their best point before they make it.
5. **Relief.** Close with the exact order sought, and the fall-back.

## Handling the bench

- **Answer the question at once and directly.** "Yes, My Lord, because ___." or "No, My Lord. The
  document at page 47 shows ___." Then go back to your structure.
- **If you do not know, say so.** "I will take instructions and come back to Your Lordship." Never
  guess a fact.
- **A hostile question is an opportunity.** It shows what is worrying the judge. Deal with that
  concern fully.
- **Do not interrupt the judge.** Let the question finish.
- **Read the bench.** If the bench is with you, stop: "If Your Lordships are with me, I will not
  take up the court's time on the other grounds." If the bench is against you on a point, move to
  your next point or your fall-back, and do not keep repeating the same submission.
- **Concede gracefully.** "I accept that ___, but that does not affect the main point, because ___."
- **Forms of address.** "My Lord" or "Your Lordship" (and "My Lady" or "Your Ladyship") are still
  widely used in the Supreme Court and the High Courts. "Your Honour" and "the Hon'ble Court" are
  also acceptable. Be consistent and respectful.
- **Never mislead.** If you later find you misstated something, correct it at the first
  opportunity. Your credibility in every future case depends on it.

## Mentioning a matter

- Be brief: the case number, the urgency in one sentence, and what you want ("listing tomorrow" or
  "an interim order until the next date").
- Have the mentioning memo and proof of service on the other side ready.

## Trial craft: examination-in-chief

- In civil cases, chief examination is usually by affidavit (Order XVIII Rule 4 CPC). Draft it in
  the witness's own words and limit it to facts they personally know.
- Mark documents as exhibits through the right witness. Get the s.63 BSA certificate for electronic
  records in advance.
- Anticipate the cross-examination. Deal with the weak points in chief so they do not look hidden.

## Trial craft: cross-examination

**Purpose.** Cross-examination either (a) gets helpful facts from the witness, or (b) damages the
witness's credibility or the reliability of their account. Decide which before you stand up. If
neither is possible, consider asking nothing, or very little.

**Rules:**

1. **Ask leading questions.** Make the statement, and let the witness agree: "You reached the shop
   at 9 p.m.?"
2. **One fact per question.** Short, simple, in plain words.
3. **Do not ask a question when you do not know the answer** unless any answer helps you.
4. **Never ask "why?"** It lets the witness explain.
5. **Do not repeat the examination-in-chief.** It only strengthens it.
6. **Build in chapters:** for example the time, the lighting, the distance, the earlier statement.
   Finish each chapter before moving on.
7. **Contradictions with an earlier statement.** First commit the witness to today's version. Then
   take them to the exact passage of the earlier statement and prove it (s.148 BSA, old s.145
   Evidence Act [VERIFY]). Statements recorded by the police during investigation can be used only
   to contradict the witness (s.181 BNSS, old s.162 CrPC).
8. **Omissions.** A significant omission from the earlier statement can amount to a contradiction.
   Bring it out precisely.
9. **Put your case.** Put your client's version to each witness who could speak to it, so it cannot
   later be said that the version was never put.
10. **Stop when you have the answer you need.** Do not ask one question too many.

**Hostile witnesses.** If your own witness turns hostile, seek permission to cross-examine them
(s.157 BSA, old s.154 Evidence Act [VERIFY]). The evidence of a hostile witness is not wiped out.
The parts that are reliable can still be used (*Khujji v. State of M.P.*, (1991) 3 SCC 627 [VERIFY]).

## Evidence objections

- **Object at the time.** An objection to the mode of proof of a document must be raised when the
  document is tendered and marked, or it may be lost. An objection to admissibility in itself (for
  example, a document that is inadmissible by law) can be raised later [VERIFY: *R.V.E. Venkatachala
  Gounder*, (2003) 8 SCC 752].
- **Common objections:** leading questions in chief, hearsay, secondary evidence without
  foundation, an electronic record without the s.63 BSA certificate, an unstamped or insufficiently
  stamped document, an unregistered document that needs registration (its admissibility for
  collateral purposes is limited), a confession to the police (s.23 BSA, old ss.25 and 26 Evidence
  Act), and a privileged communication.
- Ask the court to record the objection and the ruling in the deposition.

## Final arguments at trial

- Organise the argument by issue (civil) or by ingredient of the offence (criminal).
- For each issue or ingredient, point to the evidence: witness, page and line, and the exhibit.
- In a criminal defence, show where the prosecution failed to prove an ingredient beyond reasonable
  doubt, the contradictions and omissions, any break in the chain of circumstantial evidence
  (*Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra*, (1984) 4 SCC 116 [VERIFY]), and procedural
  breaches (for example search and seizure, sanction, or the chain of custody).
- File written arguments with a table of evidence.

## Courtroom conduct

- Arrive early. Check the cause list and the board position, and know which court hall you are in.
- Dress: black coat or jacket, white bands, and a gown where required.
- Stand when you address the court. Do not speak while the other side is addressing the court,
  except to object briefly with the court's leave.
- Address the court, not the other side.
- Be courteous to the other side, the court staff and the clerks. The bar is small and your
  reputation travels.
